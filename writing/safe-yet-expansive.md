A popular way for beginners to learn a piece of music these days is some version of scrolling piano roll notation. You know the YouTube video: little colored bars fall down the screen onto the keys you're supposed to play. The format is named for the player piano roll, where every event sits on a mechanical grid that tells you exactly what to hit and when. Its virtue is that it removes all the overhead of parsing music notation. Or of playing by ear, which, to most people, is a form of magic.

[Image: a frame from a scrolling piano-roll tutorial.]

The flipside is that this way of learning makes a song into something incredibly mechanical. Which, actually, reproduces the way many people who can read music approach learning a song. You decode which note you're supposed to hit and when, and where it is on your instrument. Then you hit it at the right time, along with all the other notes at the right time, and hopefully what comes out is music. At its worst, it feels like a game of whack-a-mole. In principle, you could listen to what comes out and verify that yes, indeed, it's music. Or at least that it somewhat resembles the song you were trying to play.

Piano roll also has some of the virtues of tablature, which swaps abstract symbolic notation for a symbol that directly represents the playing surface of the instrument. Hit this place here. The trade-off, which is big, is the same: it's purely mechanical. You could not be listening at all and still reproduce the correct notes at the correct time. You're also being handed a sequence of events with no musical context whatsoever. Nothing that matches the way we actually hear music, which is within a tonal context and in relation to a tonal center. The only context is whatever the physical design of the playing surface conveys. Music notation, by contrast, gives you exactly this tonal subset of the notes a piano roll gives you. Whatever dots fall on those lines and spaces are a much more focused subset of what you hear. A much simpler stream of information to parse.

At the other end of the spectrum from reproduce-this-event-at-this-time is something closer to improvisation, to playing by ear. As a way of learning, this approach was most thoroughly developed in the early twentieth century by the composer Carl Orff, in what became the Orff Schulwerk. It's still alive and well in Montessori, Waldorf, and other alternative primary schools. Its signature objects are the familiar wooden xylophone with removable bars, and the notion that in a pentatonic scale, anything you play sounds good. A pentatonic scale is the five notes you get from the black keys of a piano.

[Image: an Orff xylophone with some of its bars removed.]

There's a popular video that feels relevant here: Bobby McFerrin hopping around a pentatonic scale on stage, with an audience singing every note back to him intuitively before he's sung it. And of course the pentatonic scale shows up in folk traditions and ancient musical cultures the world over.

[Video: the McFerrin video.]

The ethos of the handpan invites a holistic, intuitive, and deeply human approach to exploring and learning music. The difference from the mechanical approach in any of its forms is what makes a handpan so satisfying at first: anything you play harmonizes right away. The handpan is something like a grown-up Orff instrument. And it's fundamentally different from the prevailing idea that learning music means, in some sense, learning to play the exact notes of a given song.

All of this raises real questions about what it means to learn music in our current time. Seen through a keyboard, learning music is largely about harmony and the Western tradition. Through an Ableton Push or similar controller, it's about sound design and loop-based composition. And through the handpan, it's somewhat uncharted territory.

Imagine, as a first step, a handpan whose scale harmonizes with a current popular song. The player immediately has the feeling of being part of the music, and begins to interact with it. Implicitly, they're listening actively, playing with rhythm, with song structure, with tonality and tonal center. The foundation is intuitive musicianship, and out of that experience you gradually build awareness of any number of concepts and skills. This is essentially what already happens in the handpan world. An electronic handpan expands it to a larger ecosystem of sounds, styles, and sensibilities.

So the handpan opens a real opportunity for a totally different paradigm of music learning. It's not just that the approach can be improvisational. It's also that we don't need to think about music in terms of function, in terms of chords resolving to other chords. This becomes even more powerful as popular music takes an increasingly modal turn, staying inside one scale rather than moving through changes, and handpan music points toward non-Western definitions of modality as well. So the handpan is poised to extend the basic premise of Orff pedagogy, well, far beyond the primary school classroom.
