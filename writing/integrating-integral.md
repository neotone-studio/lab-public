The first Hang scale was called Integral. PanART's name for it connotes wholeness, consonance, integration, and I think it also connotes an intention: that the notes of the instrument, and the harmonics inside each note, were meant to fit together as one resonant system. Whether any Hang ever achieved that is another question. But it's a beautiful problem, and it's worth asking what it would actually take.

Start with the craft of tuning. Bracketing whether the instrument is acoustic or digital, there's a project of getting a tonefield's fundamental to resonate at exactly the frequency you intend. That is, as close to exactly as the physical world allows. So, closer to exactly. Then, to calculate what the pure ratios of that fundamental's overtones would be, and get those to sound exactly too. And God knows, in hammered metal, there's a bit of a dance in balancing those three tones.

If that part is done, in principle, we can start to look at the relationships between tonefields. And these are multiple relationships, remember: between the fundamentals, between each of the overtones, and between the overtones and the fundamentals. Because they're a result of craft, every one of them is an independent design choice. The harmonics aren't an uncontrolled consequence of the fundamental. It's three notes in one, interacting with three notes in another.

[Image: a diagram of two tonefields, six tones, and the lines of relationship between them.]

The simplest tuning aligns the non-octave harmonic of one tonefield, its twelfth, with the fundamental of another. I imagine it has never been achieved on any acoustic handpan. Do that around the instrument and you've built a scale in pure fifths, which is to say a Pythagorean tuning, the oldest one there is. To have such a scale, rendered in the timbral vocabulary of the handpan, would be a very interesting thing to place next to its equal-tempered twin: a scale rendered just as precisely, but with the fundamentals tuned to equal temperament rather than to each other's harmonics. Setting the two next to each other would be instructive at least. This is the difference, you could say, between a pure-tuned handpan and an equal-tempered one, and you'd hear it most in the thirds.

[Video: two recordings, the same scale tuned both ways.]

This is achievable on a Neotone instrument, because extremely fine tuning can be done electronically. It could even be achieved on an acoustic instrument with the Anima, which uses electromagnets to drive the tonefields. Whether you actually want that level of precision on an acoustic instrument is another question. But let's say it's possible. Let's say the craft magic is removed, replaced with science, and the door is opened. To the pure magic of sounding ratios. Unpolluted by the pesky physical world.

Then what? When you have a baseline of craft that allows for the construction of very specific scales, you also have a way into other musical cultures and other philosophies of building a musical system. Kurd, Amara, Pygmy. Each of the names the handpan world has given its scales is a door, and each deserves a walk through.
