At the end of an unassuming street in Győrújbarát, a village in the northwest of Hungary, a very unusual instrument fills the living room of an otherwise unassuming house. Four wooden towers of three acoustic handpans each, strewn with circuit boards, cables, and mechanical strikers, snake their way to a central control unit and a MIDI keyboard. It sits somewhere between a mad scientist's version of Keith Emerson's modular Moog and an early prototype of Wintergatan's Marble Machine. It's called the Pangora. It is a profoundly strange and interesting R&D side project of MAG Instruments, one of the world's leading makers of acoustic handpans. And in a strange way, it's a project of unmaking the handpan.

[Image: the Pangora in the living room, the four towers and the keyboard.]

The Pangora, in its design, attempts to separate the handpan from some of the qualities most intrinsic to it. First and foremost: on a handpan, a single note is not a single note. That's true to a higher degree than the obvious sense in which any timbre contains many notes inside every note, or a vibrating string has many modes of vibration. The handpan designs the harmonics into the very construction of the instrument, explicitly. Each tonefield, the oval dimple you strike, is tuned to a fundamental plus its octave plus the fifth above that, all hammered in by hand. And there's crosstalk between the tonefields, each one resonating a little when its neighbors are struck. The Pangora tries to isolate the crosstalk by building an individual handpan per note, and it also tries to attenuate the harmonics.

The gesture itself is strange. It tries to reconcile the handpan with a keyboard, and in doing so, to reconcile the handpan with perhaps the most legible symbol of Western music and music theory. If something exists that can play Bach on a handpan, or that can modulate from one key to another, then the handpan is elevated, in some sense, to the level of a "real" instrument in the eyes of the tradition. Of course that isn't the stated reason for building the thing. But no doubt it's humming beneath the surface.

It's also worth mentioning that the design is already not so far from a pipe organ. An organ's mixtures are the stops that add octaves and fifths on top of the note you play, building something like an artificial additive timbre. Mixture is a good word for the handpan, and maybe the Pangora will be the occasion to introduce it.

The Pangora is also experimenting with ways to damp the harmonics of a given tonefield. It places multiple octaves of the same note on a single acoustic instrument dedicated to that note alone, and then rests its strikers on the other octaves to keep them from resonating sympathetically with the one that's struck.

A strange thought surrounds the existence of "pure tone" tonefields, especially in the higher registers. Since the octave and the twelfth are literally hammered into the metal, and result from the oval shape of the tonefield, it's possible to omit them by changing the tonefield's shape. Or, more accurately, the long axis of the oval produces the fifth, which isn't sufficiently audible in the higher notes anyway, so it gets left out of the design of those tonefields.

[Image: close-up of a tonefield, the oval and its long axis.]

The whole thing is an exercise in isolation, in separating something that is, by its nature, a linked resonant field. And in doing so it highlights what's most unique about the handpan: the things that make it evade traditional concepts, even one as basic as pitch specificity. The idea that a single tonefield represents a single note is just not quite accurate in the case of the handpan, and the traditional terminology doesn't quite capture what playing one actually produces. Meanwhile, the fact of the Pangora, and the process of making it, embeds a lot of craft knowledge and exposes a lot of the techniques that go into building an acoustic handpan.

And finally, this instrument will genuinely produce a timbre that exists on no other instrument. So we will be eagerly awaiting its first commissioned composition, and its first public concert.
