The handpan has a whole spiritual, mysterious thing going on. There's the exotic language around its different scales, and there's the real sense that every instrument sounds totally unique. So even if the notion of a scale as a reproducible set of individual notes held up, which it doesn't, there's a feeling that it's imbued with something more: the qualities of the metal, the craftsmanship of the maker, the proportions of the crosstalk and harmonics.

[Image: Gaffurio's 1492 woodcut of Pythagoras at the forge, hammers and bells and strings all labelled with their numbers.]

The origins of all of this trace back to two closely related places. One is Pythagoras, the Greek philosopher of the sixth century BC who is credited with discovering that musical harmony is a matter of simple numbers. The other is alchemy, the secretive craft of transforming metal. Pythagoras opens a few threads that the handpan picks up. The first is musical numerology: the idea that the perfect consonances are perfect because their ratios are. 2:1 for the octave, 3:2 for the fifth. The Greeks called these superparticular ratios, and the idea echoed through Plato and beyond. It's the origin of harmony-of-the-spheres thinking, of 432 Hz, of pure temperaments for sound healing. The second thread is where he supposedly discovered all this: a blacksmith's shop, listening to the clanging hammers. And that is two things at once. It's the transformation of metal, and it's embodied craft knowledge. Hephaestus, the Greek god of craft, was a blacksmith. He built Achilles' shield in the Iliad, and he's also the god who no doubt did all the plumbing repairs on Mount Olympus for Zeus.

Transformation, especially of metal, is the definition of alchemy, and there's historically a lot of secrecy surrounding it. Not necessarily because the craft was magical, but because of the role it played in some of the earliest and most valuable trade secrets. Early alchemy was especially concerned with turning non-precious metals into precious metals, so its vocabulary was heavily guarded. A language of secrecy developed among its practitioners so that the distribution of knowledge could be controlled. There's also a strong sense in which alchemy was concerned with the fundamental nature of things. The prime material, the substance that underlies all other substances. That's a metaphysical precursor to the natural sciences, perhaps never fully disentangled from them, and it dates back to the pre-Socratic philosophers. It also overlaps with the Pythagorean notion of consonance. Both are searches for what is underneath, one physical, one numerical. Which, by the way, Pythagoras probably didn't consider theoretical or separate from the physical world at all. In that way of thinking, the numbers themselves are the root of the causal chain.

All of this clearly, and literally, collides in the acoustic handpan. The original Hang was made in Bern by PanART, starting around the year 2000, and it began with an extensive and closely guarded exploration into an alchemical process of nitriding steel. They named the material Pang. This has strong echoes of the Zildjian cymbal company, whose proprietary alloy has been a closely guarded trade secret for over 400 years, kept within the family and carried from Istanbul to the United States.

Parallel to the metallurgy, PanART had to discover how to make that steel play a fundamental note with its two most superparticular overtones, the octave and the fifth above it. Metal is, by its nature, inharmonic. It doesn't vibrate at a clean frequency with orderly overtones the way a string does, which is exactly why a cymbal sounds like a crash rather than a tone. The craft of building the Hang, and every handpan since, is quite literally about transforming this inharmonic metal into something harmonic. It's done by hammering the metal into a new shape. A physical transformation that yields audible frequencies.

Now, back to the Pythagoras myth. One of its most notable qualities is that everything Pythagoras discovered that day is slightly impossible. It isn't true of metal. Hammers don't ring in the ratios of their weights. The ancient astronomer Ptolemy dismissed the story a few hundred years later. Vincenzo Galilei, Galileo's father, proved it wrong with weights and strings in 1589. And yet the myth has persisted. It has continued to resonate through culture ever since. Which means, in some sense, that the mythical aspects of the story matter more than the physical. And remember, blacksmiths in the ancient world are not exactly plumbers.

For the handpan, the question is where the mystery is located. With PanART, it began in the transformation of metal, because that's the one thing that is patentable, or at least protectable as a trade secret. It's something you can own.

[Image: an original first-generation PanART Hang.]

But the Hang caught on, and PanART kept it artificially scarce. So other makers began working out on their own how to build a similar instrument, which came to be called the handpan. And it quickly became clear that Pang, the special metal, wasn't necessary at all. Most modern handpans use a heavy press to shape a sheet of steel of the right thickness into a dome, and then the tonefields are worked into it. That's shaping, in a sense, but it isn't alchemy. So the mystery dissolves there.

[Image: a tuner's hammer on a tonefield.]

Then the mystery relocates to the craft knowledge of the tuners. As the cheap Amazon handpan illustrates, building a harmonically resonant handpan isn't simply a matter of stamping tonefields into a stamped shell. There's real craft and skill and variability in the art of tuning one, and there is no manual that can capture how to do it. It's embodied trade knowledge. It's the closest thing the handpan has to a trade secret.

So this has been a continuous relocation, a transformation, of whatever is secret in the handpan. First the material, then the craftsmanship, and then, perhaps as a result of both, the vocabulary of scales and their descriptions. Because they aren't lists of notes, whatever mass production tends to make of them. The original Hang scale was called Integral, which connotes wholeness, consonance, integration.

The thing that stopped Pythagoras in his tracks as he passed the blacksmith. For the player, this is the felt location of the mystery, its current resonation from a fundamental that dates back to prehistory. Such a thing can only be described as how it feels, not as what it is. If those things are separable at all.
