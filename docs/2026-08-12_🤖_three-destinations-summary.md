**What the site is**

Three destinations: Neotone, One, neOS. No products page, no blog, no artists page, no FAQ, no resources. The nav is centred on the instrument, with the culture on the left and the intelligence on the right, so the shape of the site says the object is what everything else is arranged around before anyone has read a word.

**What it's for**

Two things, in order. It shows a reader the way of making music the instrument comes from, and it sells them the instrument. Anything that does neither doesn't go on the site.

**Why three**

Each destination answers a state a reader is in rather than naming a category of content. Neotone is for someone asking what this world is; it holds people, materials, ideas. neOS is for someone asking how this thing thinks; it holds the craft depth, the reasoning behind the instrument's behaviour. One is for someone who has decided, and it carries the object and the purchase. Belonging and craft are things a person wants to become, which is why a piece almost never sits ambiguously between them.

This is only possible because Neotone makes one instrument. Ableton, Teenage Engineering and Roland all sell more products than a nav has slots, so their navigation must be organised by product, and everything that isn't a product gets pushed into a blog or off the domain entirely. Ableton's best teaching work lives on four separate subdomains and Loop is now a blog category. That isn't carelessness; a nav with five products in it has nowhere to put a way of thinking. Neotone's slots are free, so they're spent on what a reader wants instead of on what the company sells.

**Where the writing comes from**

This is the mechanism the whole plan depends on. The manual is the practical layer and neOS is the intelligence layer. The manual is written in three registers: the basic walkthrough, deeper functionality, and the craft questions underneath. Explaining the instrument three times at increasing depth is how its intelligence gets articulated in the first place, so writing the manual is what generates neOS material. The middle register stays in the manual; the first and last get promoted, and only when a piece holds for someone who will never touch the control it describes. A small company can't commission its way to a publication. It can notice that explaining the instrument properly already produces one.

**How it behaves**

Every page returns to its parent through the wordmark, which shrinks into a masthead as you scroll. No breadcrumbs, no back links. Opening a piece and returning restores your position in the feed. Questions are answered where they arise, which is what makes an FAQ unnecessary: lead time in the specs, what happens next under the order, can I change my mind beside the commitment.

The containers are edited, not fed. A piece can be added without going to the top, so position one is a judgement about what should meet a reader first. That's the fragile part, and worth saying plainly: the split between belonging and craft is structural and holds on its own, but the running order is a practice someone has to keep performing. Sunhouse, Teenage Engineering and Ableton all renamed or demoted a blog and inherited its behaviour without choosing it.

**How it talks**

One rule: never claim what the reader hasn't been shown. An opening exists only to gloss a name a reader can't decode, which is why One, neOS and Anima carry a line and the home page carries nothing at all and opens directly on its lead piece. A page announcing "Neotone is a new musical culture" makes the claim from outside the thing it describes; opening on a handpan player from Iran, a piece about wood, a piece about a tetrachord puts the same claim in front of someone and lets them reach it. Superlatives, unearned coinages and nameless slogans have all been tried and rejected for the same fault.

The same rule shapes the product page. Connections are listed outward from nothing, so the instrument on its own is first and a computer appears three rows down as an option. Nothing says "the centre of your setup". The ordering is the argument.

**The position it stakes**

Ableton puts a computer at the centre. Teenage Engineering makes the computer small enough to hold. Neotone makes it feel as though there is no computer. That's a claim about how music gets made, not about features, and it's the reason the editorial has to be forward rather than filed. What makes this instrument different is invisible in a photograph, so the image-first approach that works for Teenage Engineering would land it exactly where it's trying not to be, as another beautiful boutique object or another sensor controller.

**The test for anything new**

What species is it: article, container, product page, or utility. What does the reader want: belonging or craft. And does it show someone the way of making music this comes from, or help them get an instrument. If neither, it isn't on the site.
